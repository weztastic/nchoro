Inside the Nchoro AI

The computer player was told only the rules. Everything it knows, it learned by playing itself. This page runs the real AI on the board below and shows, step by step, how it looks at a position and picks a move.

Position:

1What it sees

The AI doesn't see seeds. It sees a list of 207 numbers. Each pit becomes a row of 16 switches, and the one matching its seed count is switched on. The AI always looks from the side of the player to move, so "your" pits come first. A bar shows each pit's seed count again as a plain amount. Three more numbers give both captured totals and how far the game has gone.

2Its first instinct

Those numbers go through the neural network: three layers of 256 simple units, with about 185,000 adjustable weights in total. Out come two answers at once. One is a preference for each move, which is its gut feeling. The other is how likely the player to move is to win. All this takes less than a millisecond, with no looking ahead at all.

A gut feeling can be wrong. A move that looks natural can pass through a pit with 3 seeds and hand your opponent 4. That's what the next step is for.

3Thinking ahead

Next the AI imagines possible futures. This is called Monte Carlo tree search. Each simulation starts at the current position and walks down a tree of moves. At every step it picks the move with the best score, then asks the network about the new position it reaches. That answer is passed back up the path, and every move on the way keeps a running average of how good it turned out.

The score balances two things. One is results so far, the average outcome of that move. The other is curiosity, which is big for moves the network likes but which haven't been tried much, and shrinks each time a move is visited. So the AI tests promising ideas, keeps going back to the best ones, and still checks alternatives. In a real game it runs 200 simulations per move at Strong and 800 at Full.

4The decision

After the search, the AI plays the move it explored most, not the one with the best instinct. Exploring a move many times means it kept holding up under scrutiny. Compare the two below.

5How it learned

At the start the network's weights were random, so its instinct was noise. Training ran this loop again and again:

The trick is in step 3. The network is trained to predict what its own search chose. Search is smarter than instinct, so instinct improves. A better instinct guides the next search further, and so on. The win estimate is trained on who actually won each game.

One lesson from training longer: this loop needs both sides to win sometimes. In these rules, small early mistakes hand the game to player 2. When player 2 won almost every training game, the network concluded that player 1 always loses. With every move looking equally lost, it had nothing left to learn from. Hours of extra training made it no stronger.
